You are stating what each of several candidate scientific explanations commits to
about one proposition. You are NOT judging whether the proposition is true, and you
have no literature access.

STEP 1 - Scope of the proposition, relative to the candidates.

Decide first what KIND of claim the proposition makes, compared with what the candidate
texts themselves claim:

- "within_candidates_scope": it is about the same specific systems, processes or events
  that the candidates make claims about.
- "broader_than_candidates": it is about a general class, a family of systems, a
  mechanism type, organisms in general, or other members of a class, and no candidate's
  own text makes a claim at that broader level.
- "possibility_only": it asserts only that something CAN happen, MAY happen or IS
  POSSIBLE, not that it does or did happen in the systems the candidates concern.

A candidate about what happened in one system does not determine what is true of a
whole class, and does not determine what is possible in general. Be exact here: this
scope decides whether the proposition can distinguish the candidates at all.

STEP 2 - For EVERY candidate below, answer one question: if this candidate were true,
what would it require about the proposition?

Use exactly one state per candidate:

- "positive_or_present": the candidate's own content requires the proposition, or
  makes it clearly expected. You can point to the part of the candidate that does so.
- "negative_or_absent": the candidate's own content is incompatible with the
  proposition, or makes it clearly unexpected. You can point to the part of the
  candidate that rules it out.
- "substantive_null": the candidate positively commits to NO effect, no difference
  or baseline for the specific quantity the proposition concerns. This is a
  prediction, and it needs the same kind of textual basis as the two above.
- "indeterminate": the candidate does not determine the proposition. It is
  compatible with the proposition being true AND with it being false.

Rules that matter more than anything else here:

1. Silence is not a prediction. If the candidate does not speak to the proposition,
   the state is "indeterminate". Do not infer "negative_or_absent" or
   "substantive_null" from the absence of a mention.
2. Fitting another candidate better is not incompatibility. A proposition that a
   different candidate explains more naturally is "indeterminate" for this candidate
   unless something in this candidate actually rules it out.
3. General facts are not distinguishing predictions. If the proposition is a
   general property of the system, the mechanism class or the method, and it would
   be expected whichever candidate is correct, give each candidate for which that
   holds the same state.
4. Two candidates can both be "positive_or_present". Do not create a contrast that
   the candidate texts do not contain.
5. Before choosing "negative_or_absent" or "substantive_null", identify the specific
   words in the candidate that commit to it. If you cannot, choose "indeterminate".
6. If you chose "broader_than_candidates" or "possibility_only" in STEP 1, a candidate
   is "indeterminate" unless its own text makes a claim at that same broader level or
   about that same possibility.

For a determinate state (anything except "indeterminate"), also give its strength:
"strong" (the candidate essentially entails it), "moderate" (clearly expected) or
"weak" (somewhat expected). For "indeterminate" the strength is null.
