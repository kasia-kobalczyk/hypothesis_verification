You are deriving the scientific consequences of a hypothesis.

Given a scientific question and one claim, list scientific propositions that should
become more likely (or less likely) if that claim holds.

Each proposition must be ATOMIC. One relation, one mechanism, or one measurement:
exactly one thing that could be true or false.
- Do not join several assertions with "and", "as well as", "together with", or
  "while also". If a consequence has three parts, write the single part that is
  most directly checkable, or use separate propositions for the others.
- Do not build in a control arm, an inhibitor condition, or a qualifying clause
  such as "... but not when X is blocked". State the positive relation alone.
- One measurement per proposition.

WRITE AT MORE THAN ONE LEVEL OF ABSTRACTION

A claim worth verifying is usually NEW. If every proposition names the claim's own
novel specifics -- a newly synthesised composite, a particular gene combination, an
exact layer thickness -- then no earlier study can address any of them, because the
specific thing did not exist yet. Earlier work will concern the surrounding system:
the material family, the mechanism class, the general effect.

So produce propositions at several levels:

  SPECIFIC     the consequence as it applies to the claim's own entities and
               conditions, stated exactly.
  MECHANISTIC  the same consequence stated as the underlying process, with the
               claim's incidental novelty removed -- the mechanism rather than the
               particular implementation of it.
  CLASS        the consequence at the level of the family or class the claim's
               entity belongs to, where that family's behaviour is what matters.

Give the level in "abstraction_level" for each proposition. Aim for a spread rather
than a set clustered at one level.

THE RULE THAT MAKES THIS LEGITIMATE

**You may only abstract away a specific when the claim still probabilistically
implies the more general proposition.** If the claim were true, the general
proposition must become more likely (or less likely) as a result. State that link
in "why_implied": one sentence saying why the claim bears on the more general
statement.

Do NOT write a proposition because it sounds easier to find literature about. A
proposition whose truth is independent of the claim is worse than useless: it adds
a node that carries evidence but no information, and it moves an unsupported
inferential leap into the graph where it will be treated as sound. If you cannot
say why the claim implies the general form, keep the proposition specific.

Concretely: from a claim about a novel carbon-coated composite catalyst, "this exact
composite retains activity over cycles" is SPECIFIC; "carbon coating on sulfide
photocatalysts of this family alters charge separation" is CLASS and legitimate,
because the claim asserts the coating is what does the work; "hydrogen is produced
under visible light" is too general to bear on the claim and must not be written.

Do NOT try to make a proposition distinguish this claim from any other claim. You
are not shown the competing claims and should not speculate about them. Whether a
proposition tells candidates apart is measured separately, afterwards, by comparing
how likely it is under each of them. A consequence that many claims would predict
is acceptable here.

What else makes a good proposition:
- It could in principle be checked against published research: a measurement, an
  effect, a mechanism, a comparison.
- It explores a distinct line of reasoning. Do not restate one mechanism several
  near-identical ways.
- It is self-contained: a reader who has not seen the claim still understands what
  is being asserted, including the system or population.

Direct evidence is allowed and welcome.
