You are deriving the scientific consequences of a hypothesis.

Given a scientific question and one claim, list scientific propositions that
should become more likely (or less likely) if that claim holds.

Each proposition must be ATOMIC. One relation, one mechanism, or one
measurement: exactly one thing that could be true or false.
- Do not join several assertions with "and", "as well as", "together with", or
  "while also". If a consequence has three parts, write the single part that is
  most directly checkable, or use separate propositions for the others.
- Do not build in a control arm, an inhibitor condition, or a qualifying clause
  such as "... but not when X is blocked". State the positive relation alone.
- Do not stack several quantitative conditions into one proposition. One
  measurement per proposition.
- Someone should be able to read a single published abstract and say whether it
  speaks to the proposition.

Do NOT try to make a proposition distinguish this claim from any other claim.
You are not being shown the competing claims and you should not speculate about
them. Whether a proposition tells candidates apart is measured separately,
afterwards, by comparing how likely it is under each of them. A consequence that
many claims would predict is perfectly acceptable here, and is often the most
useful kind, so write the consequences that actually follow from the claim
rather than the ones that sound most distinctive.

What makes a good proposition here:
- It is a statement about the world that could in principle be checked against
  published research: a measurement, an effect, a mechanism, a comparison.
- It explores a distinct line of reasoning. Do not restate one mechanism in
  several near-identical ways.
- It is self-contained: someone reading it without the claim should still
  understand what is being asserted, including the system or population.

Direct evidence is allowed and welcome. A proposition may restate the claim's
own observable content — there is no requirement that consequences be indirect
or remote.

Mark each proposition for whether existing literature could plausibly bear on
it ("empirically_assessable"). A proposition may be assessable whether it sits
close to the claim or several steps away.
